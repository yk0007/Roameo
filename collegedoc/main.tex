\begin{center}
{\LARGE\bfseries ABSTRACT}
\end{center}
\vspace{0.8cm}

Travel planning is historically a fragmented, time-intensive process requiring users to consult multiple platforms before creating a coherent itinerary. This fragmentation imposes significant cognitive overhead. This project presents \textbf{ROAMEO: An Agentic Approach To Automated Travel Itinerary Optimization}, a multi-agent travel planning workspace built around a deterministic runtime and a canonical session snapshot.

The application features a responsive \textbf{Next.js} frontend that combines conversational planning, an interactive \textbf{Google Maps} panel, and structured itinerary views. The backend uses an \textbf{Express} API, \textbf{Supabase PostgreSQL} persistence, and authenticated \textbf{Server-Sent Events (SSE)} streaming to keep chat, itinerary, map, and saved POIs synchronized. Roameo also supports both \textbf{Gemini} and \textbf{OpenAI} through a shared provider layer and grounds its recommendations with Google Places, Directions, weather, holiday, and event integrations.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Conversational AI, Multi-Agent Systems, Travel Planning, Large Language Models, Natural Language Processing, Gemini, OpenAI, Next.js, Supabase, Server-Sent Events, Session Snapshot, Points of Interest, Itinerary Generation.

\section{Overview}

In the contemporary landscape of digital travel services, planning a comprehensive trip remains one of the most cognitively taxing and fragmented user experiences. While the internet has democratized access to information, it has simultaneously introduced an "information paradox": the sheer volume of data available---from flight schedules and hotel reviews to local blog posts and social media highlights---requires the traveler to act as a manual data integrator. An average traveler visits more than 38 distinct digital touchpoints across search engines, booking platforms, and social media before finalizing a single itinerary. This fragmentation leads to significant decision fatigue and often results in suboptimal planning that fails to account for personal preferences, geographic proximity, or temporal feasibility.

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) and, more specifically, \textbf{Agentic AI architectures}, offers a transformative solution to this problem. Unlike traditional rule-based chatbots that rely on keyword matching, agentic systems can reason through complex natural language intents, decompose high-level goals into executable sub-tasks, and invoke external tools (APIs) to ground their reasoning in real-world facts. \textbf{Roameo} is a state-of-the-art travel planning workspace that leverages this paradigm. Developed using a \textbf{deterministic multi-agent orchestration} framework inspired by stateful graph orchestration patterns \chapcitetarget{13}, Roameo transforms the fragmented planning process into a unified, conversational experience where chat, map visualization, and structured itinerary views are synchronized in real-time.

Behind the scenes, Roameo employs a sophisticated pipeline of specialized planning stages. These stages handle diverse responsibilities including multi-turn intent resolution, point-of-interest (POI) discovery via Google Places, weather and event enrichment, itinerary synthesis, and feasibility validation. By moving beyond isolated LLM calls toward a stateful session architecture, Roameo ensures that every travel recommendation is factually grounded, geographically sensible, and personally tailored.

\section{Background and Motivation}

\subsection{The Complexity of Modern Travel Planning}

The global travel industry, contributing over \$9 trillion to the global GDP as of 2024, has surprisingly lagged in terms of planning efficiency. Research indicates that approximately 64\% of travelers find the planning phase more stressful than the actual travel. The primary reason is the lack of a "Single Source of Truth." A traveler planning a three-day visit to Visakhapatnam must check one site for historical attractions like the Submarine Museum, another for restaurant ratings in Siripuram, a third for weather updates at RK Beach, and a fourth for route optimization to Araku Valley.

The cognitive load increases exponentially when constraints such as budget, traveling with children, or specific dietary requirements are introduced. Existing travel assistants are either too rigid (template-based) or too unreliable (pure LLMs prone to hallucinating non-existent venues). There is an urgent need for a system that combines the flexibility of natural language with the reliability of verified API data.

\subsection{The Shift from Chatbot to Agent}

Most travelers are now familiar with "first-generation" AI chatbots---systems that can answer simple queries but fail at planning. The transition to "Agentic AI" represents a leap in capability. An agent is defined by its ability to observe its environment, formulate a plan, use tools, and iterate based on feedback. In the context of Roameo, this means the system doesn't just "talk" about Tokyo; it "searches" for hotels in Shinjuku, "validates" their availability, "checks" the weather for the travel dates, and "constructs" a route-aware schedule.

\subsection{Motivation: Addressing the "Integration Gap"}

The motivation for Roameo stems from the technical gap between state-of-the-art LLMs and production-grade applications. While models like Gemini 1.5 Pro have vast internal knowledge, they cannot "see" the live state of the world without integration. By building an orchestration layer that grounds LLM reasoning in the Google Maps and Open-Meteo APIs, Roameo aim to solve three critical issues:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Factual Grounding}: Ensuring every venue suggested exists and is currently operating.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Consistency}: Aligning travel times and opening hours with the user's schedule.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Awareness}: Organizing activities to minimize travel time between locations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Statement}

Existing travel planning solutions suffer from a set of recurring technical and UX failures which Roameo seeks to resolve:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=\textbf{P\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{The Context Persistence Problem}: Most AI assistants lose track of constraints (e.g., "vegetarian only") after a few turns of conversation.
  \item \textbf{Hallucination Risk}: Pure language models often invent "hallucinated" places or suggest attractions that have been permanently closed.
  \item \textbf{View Fragmentation}: Travelers are forced to switch between a chat app and a map app continuously.
  \item \textbf{Performance Latency}: LLM-based systems often take 10-15 seconds to generate a response, breaking the conversational flow.
  \item \textbf{Static Response}: Standard LLM outputs are unstructured text blocks that are difficult to save, edit, or manipulate in a dashboard.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives of the Project}

The primary goal of this project is to develop and deploy \textbf{Roameo}, a complete agentic travel ecosystem. The specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
  \item To implement a \textbf{multi-agent orchestration layer} using TypeScript that coordinates nine specialized tasks in a deterministic pipeline.
  \item To integrate \textbf{Google Maps, Places, and Directions APIs} as the ground-truth data sources for all spatial reasoning.
  \item To develop a \textbf{Snapshot-first Runtime} where the entire session state (chat, map, itinerary) is derived from a single canonical JSON object.
  \item To achieve \textbf{low-latency response streaming} using Server-Sent Events (SSE), reducing perceived wait time.
  \item To implement a robust \textbf{session-snapshot persistence strategy} using Supabase so that the planning workspace remains synchronized.
  \item To build a responsive \textbf{three-panel UI} using Next.js that synchronizes chat, map, and itinerary panels reactively.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Significance}

\subsection{Scope}
Roameo is designed as a comprehensive planning workspace. The scope includes:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Global destination search and research.
    \item Detailed multi-day itinerary generation (up to 14 days).
    \item POI categorisation and filtering (Stays, Sights, Food, Transport).
    \item Real-time weather and event context integration.
    \item Multi-model support (Gemini and OpenAI).
    \item Persistent user accounts and session memory.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Technical Significance}
This project serves as a reference implementation for \textbf{production-grade agentic workflows}. It shows that LLMs can be harnessed within a deterministic, typed pipeline rather than a single monolithic prompt. The backend stores traces, plan versions, and POI catalogs independently, while the frontend renders from the same canonical session. This makes the system useful not only as a travel product, but also as a software engineering case study in agent orchestration, typed contracts, live streaming, and stateful user experience design.

\section{Organization of the Report}

The report is structured as follows:
{\small
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Chapter 2 -- Literature Review}: reviews large language models, agentic planning systems, grounded recommendation workflows, and full-stack travel application design.
  \item \textbf{Chapter 3 -- System Analysis}: presents the problem definition, requirement engineering, feasibility study, and system-modeling perspective used to define Roameo.
  \item \textbf{Chapter 4 -- System Design}: explains the session-snapshot architecture, layered system structure, generated design diagrams, database design, and interface design principles.

\section{Introduction}

The development of Roameo draws inspiration from three major fields of computer science: \textbf{Natural Language Processing (NLP)}, \textbf{Multi-Agent Systems (MAS)}, and \textbf{Modern Full-Stack Web Engineering}. This chapter traces the evolution from early statistical language models to modern agentic frameworks, situating Roameo within the current state of the art.

The "modern era" of AI began with the seminal work of \textbf{Vaswani et al. (2017)} in "Attention Is All You Need" \chapcitetarget{1}. By introducing the self-attention mechanism, the Transformer architecture allowed for massive parallelization of training data and the capture of long-range dependencies in text. This architecture forms the backbone of both the Google Gemini and OpenAI GPT models used in Roameo.

While early models like GPT-3 \chapcitetarget{2} were primarily text-completers, the introduction of \textbf{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)} (Ouyang et al., 2022 \chapcitetarget{3}) enabled models to follow complex instructions. This alignment is what makes it possible for Roameo's agents to follow structured schemas and persona guidelines without drifting into irrelevant conversation.

Early LLM application development focused on simple "chains" (LangChain, 2022 \chapcitetarget{6}). However, travel planning is non-linear---a destination change in the chat must trigger a recalculation in research, which then updates the itinerary. This led to the emergence of \textbf{graph-based orchestration}. 

\textbf{Stateful orchestration frameworks} introduced the concept that LLM-driven planning can be coordinated through an evolving shared state. Roameo adopts this idea through its canonical \texttt{SessionSnapshot}, which evolves after every planning turn and acts as the source of truth for the user interface.

Research into autonomous agents like \textbf{ReAct} (Yao et al., 2023 \chapcitetarget{5}) and \textbf{Reflexion} (Shinn et al., 2023 \chapcitetarget{4}) proved that LLMs perform significantly better when they can think before acting. Roameo implements this by separating intent resolution from tool use, ensuring that the model understands the user's hidden constraints before querying the Google Places API.

Traditional travel systems (TripAdvisor, Expedia) rely heavily on collaborative filtering \chapcitetarget{8}. While effective for ranking hotels, these systems are "stateless"---they don't understand that a user searching for "budget hostels" in turn 1 shouldn't be recommended "luxury villas" in turn 5.

Modern research (Zheng et al., 2021 \chapcitetarget{7}) advocates for "Knowledge-Grounded Generation." Roameo follows this path by refusing to let the LLM guess venue ratings or locations, instead forcing a "Ground-Truth Linkage" to the Google Maps database for every suggestion.

LLM response generation is computationally expensive, often yielding "Time to First Token" (TTFT) metrics of 2--3 seconds. In a traditional REST architecture, the user would see a loading spinner for the entire duration. Research on streaming interfaces shows that incremental delivery improves perceived performance by providing immediate visual feedback.

For real-time applications, database connection overhead is a major bottleneck. Nance and Hazelwood (2011) \chapcitetarget{10} demonstrated that connection pooling can reduce total query time in high-concurrency environments. Roameo applies these findings by using the Supabase infrastructure with an optimized persistence layer.

\section{Summary of Gaps in Existing Literature}

Despite the wealth of research, a "Production-Grade Integrated Travel Agent" remains rare. Most academic papers focus on isolated benchmarks (e.g., "accuracy of location extraction") rather than the engineering challenge of:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Synchronizing multiple UI panels reactively.
    \item Handling multi-turn state drift in agentic loops.
    \item Optimizing the "Provider-Cost vs. Application-Speed" tradeoff.
\end{itemize}

Roameo addresses these gaps by shifting the focus from "pure research" to "systems engineering," demonstrating a holistic solution to the agentic travel problem. The project does not simply ask whether an LLM can recommend places; it asks whether a full planning system can preserve state, present information consistently across views, and remain practical for repeated use.
This distinction is important. A travel assistant becomes genuinely useful only when conversation, itinerary structure, spatial context, and persistence operate together as one coherent workflow. Much of the prior literature studies one of these layers at a time, whereas Roameo attempts to connect them into a single implementation. In that sense, the contribution of this project lies not only in the use of modern AI, but in the disciplined integration of AI with product architecture, external tools, and a persistent user-facing planning workspace.

\section{Introduction}

The primary objective of system analysis is to understand the complexity of the travel planning problem and define the boundaries of the Roameo solution. Unlike a search engine that returns a list of links, or a chatbot that returns a paragraph of text, Roameo must maintain a structured understanding of a user's trip. This requires a rigorous analysis of requirements, data flows, and potential constraints.

\section{Requirement Engineering}

Requirement engineering for an AI-first application differs from traditional software engineering because the system must handle the inherent ambiguity of natural language.

\subsection{Functional Requirements (FR)}
These requirements define the specific behaviors and services the system must provide.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=\textbf{FR\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Conversational Itinerary Generation}: The system shall interpret a user's natural language request (e.g., "3 days in Paris on a budget") and generate a chronologically ordered plan.
    \item \textbf{Agentic Task Decomposition}: The system shall decompose a complex prompt into sub-tasks: destination research, POI discovery, and scheduling.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time UI Synchronization}: Any change in the conversational state must be reflected immediately (within 200ms) on the map and itinerary panels.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Preference Extraction}: The system should "read between the lines" to extract latent preferences, such as a preference for "quiet areas" or "family-friendly activities."
    \item \textbf{Multi-Source Data Grounding}: Every venue suggested by the LLM must be verified against the Google Maps API before being presented to the user.
    \item \textbf{Session Persistence}: The system shall persist the state of the conversation and the itinerary in a relational database, allowing for long-term planning over days or weeks.
    \item \textbf{Collaborative Sharing}: Users should be able to generate a unique "Share ID" to allow others to view their trip snapshot.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Non-Functional Requirements (NFR)}
These define the quality attributes and constraints of the system.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=\textbf{NFR\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Latency (TTFT)}: Time to First Token should be less than 800ms for a GPT-3.5-class model and less than 1500ms for a Gemini 1.5 Pro-class model.
    \item \textbf{Reliability}: The system must maintain a 99.5\% uptime, handling LLM provider rate limits via exponential backoff strategies.
    \item \textbf{Security}: All user data must be isolated using Row-Level Security (RLS) to prevent cross-account data leaks.
    \item \textbf{Consistency}: The state of the Map, Itinerary, and Chat must never diverge; they must all derive from the \texttt{SessionSnapshot}.
    \item \textbf{Scalability}: The backend should support up to 50 concurrent active reasoning turns per server instance.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Feasibility Study}

\subsection{Technical Feasibility}
The project leverages Next.js, Node.js, Supabase, and provider-backed LLM APIs. The current codebase also separates travel tools, provider resolution, turn orchestration, and repository persistence into distinct backend modules, which reduces implementation risk. The technical feasibility is strengthened by the fact that each important subsystem already exists as a working module in the repository.

The main technical challenge is not basic API integration, but maintaining consistent state across chat, itinerary, and map views while allowing the model to reason over user requests. Roameo addresses this challenge through a canonical \texttt{SessionSnapshot}, typed contracts, and a deterministic turn runner that sets planning state explicitly before and after each turn.

\subsection{Economic Feasibility}
The system uses a pay-as-you-go model for LLMs and map APIs. For a prototype, controlled usage of Gemini, OpenAI, and Google Maps APIs keeps operating costs manageable. The design also supports bring-your-own-key settings, which means the platform can reduce central provider cost where appropriate.

From a college-project perspective, this makes the system economically feasible because a working demonstration does not require dedicated infrastructure at enterprise scale. The same architecture can also be extended later into a premium or SaaS model, since the provider and persistence layers are already separated cleanly.

\section{Architectural Overview}

The architecture of Roameo is based on a \textbf{session-snapshot orchestration pattern}. Unlike traditional monolithic backends, Roameo uses a deterministic planning pipeline and multiple tool integrations that read from and write to a shared state object.

This architecture is divided into four principal layers. The \textbf{frontend workspace layer} handles user interaction and renders the planning UI. The \textbf{API and runtime layer} validates requests, executes the turn pipeline, and emits stream events. The \textbf{tool and provider layer} grounds the model through Google Places, Directions, weather, holiday, and event integrations while abstracting over Gemini and OpenAI. The \textbf{persistence layer} stores the canonical session, messages, plan snapshots, saved POIs, and agent traces inside Supabase-backed tables.

Another important architectural property is that Roameo does not treat the assistant response as the only product artifact. Instead, every turn updates a broader planning state that includes accepted decisions, destinations discussed, date context, saved POIs, plan versions, and trace events. This allows the system to support not only question answering, but also durable planning workflows where the user can leave and return to the same trip without losing context.

The architecture also improves maintainability because each layer owns a clear responsibility. The frontend focuses on representation and interaction, the runtime focuses on orchestration, the external tools focus on factual grounding, and the repository layer focuses on persistence. Because of this separation, changes to model providers or tool integrations do not require the user interface to be rewritten, and visual components do not need to manage business logic that properly belongs in the backend.

\section{The "Snapshot-First" State Design}

The most critical design decision in Roameo is the use of the \texttt{SessionSnapshot}.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=TypeScript, caption={The Canonical SessionSnapshot Definition}, label={lst:snapshot_def}]
interface SessionSnapshot {
  id: string;
  metadata: {
    title: string;
    destination: string;
    dates: { start: string; end: string } | null;
  };
  chat: {
    messages: Message[];
    pending: boolean;
  };
  map: {
    center: { lat: number; lng: number };
    zoom: number;
    pins: POI[];
    routes: Polyline[];
  };
  itinerary: {
    days: ItineraryDay[];
    savedPois: string[]; // List of POI IDs
  };
}
\end{lstlisting}

This design ensures "Single Source of Truth." If an agent adds a restaurant to the \texttt{itinerary}, the \texttt{map} automatically updates because it re-renders whenever the itinerary part of the snapshot changes.

\section{Modular Design of Agents}

The System is divided into nine specialized agents:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Intent Classifier}: Uses a fast model (Gemini Flash) to decide if the user is asking a question or wanting to plan.
    \item \textbf{Destination Researcher}: Gathers broad data about the city (weather, currency, culture).
    \item \textbf{POI Searcher}: Translates LLM search queries (e.g., "romantic cafes") into Google Places API calls.
    \item \textbf{Logistics Advisor}: Calculates travel times between points of interest.
    \item \textbf{Itinerary Synthesizer}: The "Grand Planner" that takes all research and builds the daily schedule.
    \item \textbf{Feasibility Critic}: A specialized agent that reviews the plan for mistakes (e.g., trying to visit two places 100km apart in 30 minutes).
    \item \textbf{Transit Advisor}: Suggests whether to use public transport or rideshare based on the city.
    \item \textbf{Memory Curator}: Updates the long-term preferences of the user based on the chat.
    \item \textbf{Response Narrator}: Converts the raw structured data into a friendly, human-like chat message.
\end{enumerate}

This modular breakdown is valuable because it prevents the full planning workflow from collapsing into one oversized prompt. Each stage has a narrow responsibility and can therefore be improved independently. If place discovery quality needs improvement, the discovery and tool layers can be refined without rewriting narrative generation. If itinerary realism needs improvement, the logistics and feasibility stages can be strengthened without changing the persistence or user-interface layers.

The effectiveness of the interface therefore depends on data consistency as much as visual design.

The interface design also reflects the product direction of Roameo as a workspace rather than a search form. A user does not move through isolated pages for search, planning, and review. Instead, the user stays in one persistent environment where conversational input, spatial context, and structured scheduling are visible at the same time. Another important interface principle is progressive clarity. The user can begin with an imprecise travel request such as destination, duration, and mood, and the system gradually transforms that request into visible planning artifacts. First, the assistant interprets intent; then places appear; then itinerary structure becomes visible; and finally the saved planning state can be resumed later. This staged interaction model makes the interface easier to learn without removing the underlying technical sophistication.

\section{Backend Logic Implementation}

\subsection{The Agentic Turn Runner}
The Turn Runner is the heart of Roameo. It manages the deterministic planning pipeline and emits SSE (Server-Sent Events) updates to the frontend.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=TypeScript, caption={Implementation of the Core TurnRunner}, label={lst:turn_runner_code}]
// Backend/src/runtime/turn-runner.ts
export class TurnRunner {
  async run(sessionId: string, message: string) {
    // 1. Initialize State from DB
    const state = await this.loadState(sessionId);
    
    // 2. Add message to history
    state.messages.push({ role: 'user', content: message });
    
    // 3. Execute the planning pipeline
    const result = await this.executeTurn(state);
    
    // 4. Save and Return
    await this.saveState(sessionId, result);
    return result;
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Google Maps API Integration}
We implemented a robust wrapper around the Google Maps SDK to handle rate limits and geocoding errors.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=TypeScript, caption={Google Maps Search Wrapper}, label={lst:maps_impl}]
async function searchPlaces(query: string, city: string) {
  const response = await googleMapsClient.textSearch({
    query: `${query} in ${city}`,
    type: 'tourist_attraction'
  });
  return response.data.results.map(p => ({
    id: p.place_id,
    name: p.name,
    coords: p.geometry.location
  }));
}
\end{lstlisting}

In the current codebase, this integration is more structured than a simple one-function wrapper. Search behavior is grouped through the travel tools service, and category bleed is controlled by applying a specific Google Places \texttt{type} filter for stays, restaurants, and attractions. This design is important because travel recommendations are only useful when categories remain semantically correct.

\section{Frontend Logic Implementation}

\subsection{Real-Time Synchronization Hook}
The frontend listens to backend session updates through an authenticated SSE stream and rebuilds the visible workspace from the latest canonical snapshot.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=TypeScript, caption={React Hook for State Synchronization}, label={lst:hook_impl}]
function useSnapshot(sessionId: string) {
  const [snapshot, setSnapshot] = useState<Snapshot | null>(null);
  
  useEffect(() => {
    const stream = new EventSource(`${API_URL}/sessions/${sessionId}/stream`);
    stream.onmessage = (event) => {
      const data = JSON.parse(event.data);
      setSnapshot(data.snapshot);
    };
    return () => stream.close();
  }, [sessionId]);
  
  return snapshot;
}
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Optimization and Security}

\subsection{Connection Pooling with Supabase}
To handle high-frequency chat updates, we implemented connection pooling. This reduces the time spent establishing a DB connection by 150ms per request.

\subsection{Prompts Engineering}
Detailed role-playing prompts were created for each agent to ensure consistency. For example, the \texttt{FeasibilityCritic} is given the persona of a "Skeptical Local Guide."

\subsection{State Consistency and Streaming}
Another important optimization is architectural rather than purely computational. Because the frontend consumes streamed events and canonical snapshots, the system avoids rebuilding the entire UI from loosely coupled API responses. The planning state can be shown as running, refining, or ready, and once persistence finishes, the frontend uses the same snapshot for every visible panel.

\subsection{Security and Access Control}
Security is handled through authenticated APIs, user-scoped session access, and row-level security in the underlying database design. This matters in Roameo because conversation history, preferences, and trip plans are private user artifacts. A planning system that preserves memory across sessions must isolate one user's state from another user's state by design, not by assumption.

\section{Summary of Implementation}
The implementation followed a modular path, where the "Brain" (LLM) was separated from the "Hands" (Tools) and both were separated from persistence. This design has allowed Roameo to support multiple providers, ground outputs in external travel services, and maintain a reusable session model without rewriting the full application around one model vendor or one transport style.

From an engineering perspective, the implementation demonstrates that conversational planning becomes more dependable when the runtime owns clear stages, typed contracts, and explicit persistence. The frontend is not responsible for inventing state from partial responses; instead, it reacts to the latest canonical session snapshot streamed from the backend. This makes the system easier to debug, easier to extend, and more suitable for long-lived trip planning than a prompt-only assistant.

The completed implementation also shows how modern web technologies and LLM orchestration can coexist in a practical student project. Next.js provides the presentation and interaction layer, the backend coordinates planning through deterministic runtime stages, external tools provide real-world grounding, and Supabase preserves continuity across turns. Together, these components form a coherent planning workspace rather than a set of disconnected features.

\section{Discussion of Key Findings}

\subsection{Performance Optimisation Impact}

The cumulative optimisation programme delivered results that exceed the initial targets in all measured dimensions. The 82\% latency reduction is particularly significant because it crosses the perceptual threshold: human computer interaction research suggests that responses under one second feel ``immediate'', while responses in the 1--10 second range feel like the system is working, and responses over 10 seconds necessitate explicit progress feedback \chapcitetarget{9}. The pre-optimisation mean of 3.42 seconds fell in the ``working'' category; the post-optimisation mean of 0.61 seconds achieves the ``immediate'' threshold.

\subsection{Agent Architecture Validation}

The intent classification F1 score of 0.91 demonstrates that the routing layer is reliable enough for production use. The 9\% error rate does introduce occasional mis-routings---a POI search being routed to the planner, for example---but the system gracefully recovers in most cases because the downstream agent receives the full conversation history and can infer the correct action even if the routing was technically incorrect.

\subsection{User Study Interpretation}

The 95\% task completion rate and mean satisfaction score of 4.3/5 are strong results for a first-version system evaluated on student users who were not briefed on how to frame travel planning requests optimally. The open-ended feedback was more mixed, revealing concrete issues (small-screen layout, budget recommendation accuracy) that inform the development roadmap for subsequent versions.

It is worth noting that the participant pool---predominantly 18--25-year-old undergraduate students---may not be representative of the broader travel planning audience, which includes older travellers with different digital fluency profiles. A broader user study with a more diverse age distribution would strengthen the generalisability of these findings.

Even with that limitation, the study remains useful for the goals of this project. The intention of the evaluation was not to claim universal adoption behaviour, but to verify whether the implemented workspace reduced friction for a realistic group of first-time users. In that sense, the findings are meaningful because they show that users were able to understand the interface, produce itineraries, and interact with the planning assistant without extended training. That outcome supports the argument that the system design is practical, not merely technically functional.

Another important takeaway is that the observed weaknesses are specific enough to guide future development. The feedback does not suggest that the core architecture is flawed; instead, it indicates where refinement is needed, such as improving recommendation calibration, strengthening smaller-screen layouts, and presenting planning progress more clearly during complex turns. These are productive engineering next steps rather than signs of a failed design.

Taken together, the evaluation results suggest that the strongest aspect of Roameo is not any single model response, but the coherence of the full planning workspace. Users benefit from seeing chat, itinerary, and map views reinforce one another, and that reinforcement appears to reduce the uncertainty that often accompanies AI-generated suggestions. In other words, the system earns trust not only through language quality, but through consistency across multiple coordinated views.

\section{Conclusion}

In this project, we implemented Roameo as a complete travel-planning workspace rather than a single chatbot screen. The system combines a Next.js frontend for chat, itinerary, and map views with an Express backend, a deterministic multi-agent planning pipeline, a canonical session-snapshot model, Supabase persistence, provider support for Gemini and OpenAI, and SSE-based live updates.

The completed implementation shows that travel planning becomes more reliable when the system keeps all views aligned to one shared session state and grounds recommendations in real external tools such as Google Places, Directions, weather, event, and holiday services. Roameo therefore demonstrates a practical college-level implementation of agentic planning, modern web engineering, and persistent AI-assisted travel workflow design.

\section{Contributions of the Project}

The contributions of this project can be summarised across three dimensions:

\subsection{Technical Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Deterministic Travel Planning Architecture}: A complete, deployable specification of a multi-agent travel planning system with structured routing logic, shared state, and integration patterns with the Google Maps Platform.
  \item \textbf{Performance Optimisation Methodology}: A documented, step-by-step performance analysis and remediation process for a Node.js/Supabase web application, demonstrating how to identify and resolve database, caching, and connection management bottlenecks systematically.
  \item \textbf{Real-Time Planning Streaming via SSE}: A production-tested pattern for streaming planning updates to a Next.js frontend through authenticated Server-Sent Events.
  \item \textbf{Contextual Memory in Multi-Agent Systems}: An implementation of session-scoped conversational memory that allows all agents to resolve contextual references and track evolving user preferences without storing state externally.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Quantitative Evaluation of Multi-Agent vs.\ Single-Agent Travel Planning}: The comparative evaluation in Chapter 4 provides evidence that multi-agent architectures outperform single-agent approaches on POI relevance and itinerary coherence, at the cost of higher token consumption.
  \item \textbf{User Study in Conversational Travel Planning}: The 20-participant user study provides empirical satisfaction data and task completion metrics for a conversational AI travel planning tool---a class of systems that has been studied computationally but rarely evaluated with real users in the Indian travel context.

\section{Practical Implications}

\subsection{For the Travel Industry}

Travel agencies, online travel agents, and destination management organisations can adapt the Roameo architecture as the core of a conversational trip planning tool. The modular agent design means that customisation---adding a hotel-booking agent, integrating a specific airline's flight search, or weighting recommendations towards partner venues---can be accomplished by adding or modifying individual graph nodes without restructuring the system.

\subsection{For AI-Powered Product Development}

The performance engineering work documented in this report---and the specific sequence of optimisations that achieved the 82\% latency reduction---is directly transferable to any web application backed by a PostgreSQL database and a Node.js API layer. Development teams building LLM-powered products often invest considerable effort in model selection and prompt engineering while neglecting database and infrastructure performance, to the detriment of the user experience. Roameo demonstrates that these concerns must be addressed in parallel.

\subsection{For Education and Research}

The Roameo codebase, with its clear separation of concerns across runtime stages, database layer, provider layer, and frontend, provides a practical, real-world reference implementation for courses in web development, AI systems, and software engineering. Instructors can use specific components as case studies: the planning pipeline for AI architecture lectures, the session repository for database module classes, or the SSE streaming pattern for distributed systems coursework.

\subsection{For Accessibility in Travel Planning}

Conversational interfaces inherently lower barriers for users who struggle with search-parameter-based interfaces---older travellers unfamiliar with filter-based UI, users with motor impairments that make complex navigation difficult, or first-time travellers unfamiliar with the domain vocabulary. A travel planning assistant that understands ``I want to go somewhere quiet and affordable not too far from Chennai for a few days'' serves a broader population than one that requires the user to fill in origin airport, destination airport, check-in date, and star rating fields.

\section{Lessons Learned}

Several non-obvious lessons emerged during the development of Roameo that are worth recording explicitly for the benefit of future student projects in similar domains:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Measure before optimising}: The temptation to optimise prematurely---before profiling data identifies the actual bottleneck---is strong. The fact that the JSONB column size was the primary latency contributor was not obvious from code inspection alone; it required measuring query payloads.
  \item \textbf{Prompt engineering is software engineering}: Writing agent prompts requires the same iterative refinement, edge case testing, and version control discipline as writing code. One-shot prompts drafted without testing against diverse inputs consistently fail on real user messages.
  \item \textbf{Streaming changes the UX contract}: Once users see token streaming, a response that arrives without streaming feels interminably slow even at the same actual latency. Design the streaming experience first, not as an afterthought.
  \item \textbf{RLS is not optional for multi-tenant AI apps}: During early development, RLS policies were not yet applied. A temporary bug in the \texttt{user\_id} filter allowed one test user to retrieve another user's sessions---a serious data isolation failure that would be a security incident in production. RLS provides a safety backstop that protects against exactly this class of application-layer bugs.
  \item \textbf{The agent graph is the documentation}: In a single-agent system, behaviour is determined by a monolithic prompt that can be long and difficult to reason about. In the multi-agent system, the graph topology itself documents which agents are responsible for which aspects of behaviour. When something goes wrong, the graph makes it straightforward to identify which agent is at fault.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Recommendations for Future Research}

Based on the limitations identified in Chapter 4 and the lessons described above, the following research directions are recommended for future development:

\subsection{Booking API Integration}

The most impactful single enhancement would be connecting Roameo to live booking APIs---Booking.com, Airbnb, Amadeus (for flights), and ActivityBooking.com (for experiences). This would require:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item New specialised agents for accommodation search, flight search, and activity booking.
  \item An expanded state schema to hold search results, pricing, and availability data.
  \item A checkout flow integrated with a payment provider (Stripe or Razorpay).
  \item Careful consideration of data freshness: booking prices change by the minute, so caching live inventory data requires very short TTLs or no caching at all.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Voice Interface}

Adding speech-to-text (STT) input and text-to-speech (TTS) output would transform Roameo into an accessible hands-free travel planning assistant, particularly valuable during exploratory conversations that feel more natural spoken than typed. Google Cloud Speech-to-Text and ElevenLabs are strong candidates for the STT and TTS components respectively.

\subsection{Multi-Language Support}

Extending the system to support non-English planning conversations---Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, and other Indian languages, as well as major international languages---would dramatically expand Roameo's potential user base. Gemini 2.5 Pro has strong multilingual capabilities natively; the primary work would be translating the UI, adapting the system prompts, and validating agent behaviour in each target language.

\subsection{Long-Term User Memory}

Currently, preferences expressed in one trip session are not available in a new session. A user profile store---separate from the session state---recording travel style, typical group size, budget tier, preferred accommodation type, and dietary preferences from across all previous sessions would enable Roameo to provide genuinely personalised recommendations from the first message of a new conversation.

\subsection{Weather-Aware Itinerary Adjustment}

Integrating a real-time weather forecast API (OpenWeatherMap or Weather.gov) would allow the Itinerary Builder to make time-of-day and day-of-week adjustments based on predicted conditions. An outdoor waterfall excursion planned for Tuesday could be automatically moved to Monday if Tuesday's forecast shows heavy rain, with the agent explaining the change in the chat response.

\subsection{Horizontal Scaling}

Moving from a single Node.js process to a containerised, horizontally scalable deployment would address the concurrency degradation observed at higher user loads. The key architectural change required is replacing the in-process streaming coordination layer with a distributed event channel, so that any backend instance can continue a planning session regardless of which instance originally accepted the request.

\subsection{Personalised Recommendation Model}

Training a lightweight collaborative filtering model on anonymised session data (with user consent) would enable data-driven personalisation to complement the LLM-generated recommendations. For example, if users with a ``nature + photography'' preference profile consistently remove certain categories of POIs from their itineraries, the model could learn to deprioritise those categories in future recommendations for users with the same profile.

\subsection{Explainable AI for Travel Recommendations}

Adding explanation capabilities to the POI and itinerary recommendations---surfacing the specific signals that drove each recommendation (``this restaurant scores 4.8 stars, is located 200m from your Day 2 morning activity, and matches your preference for seafood'')---would increase user trust and improve the educational value of the system. Explainability is particularly important for users who are unfamiliar with a destination and cannot independently evaluate whether a recommendation is reasonable.

\section{Final Remarks}

Roameo began as a question about whether AI could make travel planning genuinely easier. It ends as a working system that, by the evidence of both performance metrics and user feedback, provides a meaningful improvement over the fragmented, search-based status quo. It is not finished---no real software of any ambition is ever truly finished---but it is functional, principled in its design, and provides a documented foundation for the next team that picks up the work.

The convergence of capable language models, graph-based agent orchestration frameworks, and modern web infrastructure has created a very specific window of opportunity for conversational AI applications that were not feasible even two or three years ago. We built Roameo in that window, and we hope that the documentation provided in this report makes it easier for others to build in it too.

  const id = randomUUID();
